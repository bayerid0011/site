Liquidity Mining Traps: How Token Approvals Can Expose a Web3 Wallet

What ERC-20 approve/allowance does, why unlimited permissions matter and how a malicious dApp can abuse an approval.

By Financial Safety Research TeamLast reviewed: October 2026 · 8–11 min read
THE PROBLEM

Connecting a wallet does not automatically let a website move every token. The critical step is usually what the user signs. For ERC-20 tokens, an approval can give a spender permission to transfer a defined allowance; unlimited allowances increase exposure if the spender is malicious or compromised.

Anatomy of the mechanism

Connection is not approval

A wallet connection mainly exposes public address information. Spending normally requires a signed transaction or previously granted allowance.

Read the approval transaction

Inspect token, spender address and allowance. A site may describe the action as enabling mining while the wallet actually shows an approval.

Unlimited allowances persist

An allowance can remain active after the user leaves the site, giving the spender continued authority within the approved amount.

Trace later transferFrom events

Explorer logs can show which spender initiated transfers even if the user did not sign each later movement.

Revoke unused permissions

Revoking an allowance is preventive and does not undo transfers already confirmed on-chain.

Legal and regulatory analysis

Applicable framework

Public blockchain records can establish transaction hashes, addresses and token movements, but they usually do not establish the legal identity behind an address. Tracing is evidence; it is not a promise of recovery.

What the rule does not mean

Smart-contract and exchange regulation is jurisdiction-specific. A wallet-to-wallet transfer does not acquire card-network reversal rights merely because the underlying representation was deceptive.

Practical compliance point

Where funds touch a regulated custodian or exchange, official reporting and AML processes may be relevant, but any freeze, disclosure or return depends on law, evidence and timing.

Evidence that matters

Build a chronological file another person can follow without relying on memory. Preserve originals where possible.

  • Wallet address
  • Approval transaction hash
  • Spender contract
  • Token-transfer hashes
  • Domain and signed-message history

Practical next steps

  • Revoke unnecessary approvals.
  • Move remaining assets if compromise is suspected.
  • Preserve hashes and spender addresses.
  • Report identified custodial touchpoints through official channels.

What not to do

  • Do not sign another approval just to undo the first unless you understand it.
  • Never provide a seed phrase to support.
  • Do not confuse wallet connection with legal identity verification.
