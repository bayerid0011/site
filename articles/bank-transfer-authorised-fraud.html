Bank Transfer Liability: Can You Reverse a Wire Payment If You Were Deceived?

A completed authorised transfer is not a cancel button. The bank needs beneficiary data, chronology and the correct process.

By Financial Safety Research TeamLast reviewed: October 2026 · 8–11 min read
THE PROBLEM

A customer may personally approve a transfer because they believe an investment, invoice or beneficiary is genuine. When the deception is discovered later, the payment can still be authorised in a technical sense even though the decision to send it was induced by false information. That distinction matters when speaking to the bank.

Anatomy of the mechanism

Describe authorisation accurately

Tell the bank whether you initiated the payment. If you did, explain the alleged deception rather than calling it an unauthorised debit.

Give complete beneficiary data

Provide name, IBAN/account number, BIC/SWIFT if available, amount, currency, date and reference.

Ask the bank to identify the process

Depending on the rail and status, the bank may discuss recall, request for return, fraud report, trace or complaint.

Document the discovery date

State what changed your understanding of the transfer: beneficiary mismatch, false identity, non-provision or a new payment demand.

Track every bank response

Keep case numbers, dates and written decisions for any later complaint or review.

Legal and regulatory analysis

Applicable framework

PSD2 remains the current EU payment-services framework as of October 2026. PSD3 and the Payment Services Regulation reached political agreement and are progressing toward final adoption; this site does not treat the future framework as already in force.

What the rule does not mean

The EPC SEPA Credit Transfer scheme contains a formal Recall procedure for defined reasons. The originator PSP initiates it; a recall request is not a guarantee that funds will be returned.

Practical compliance point

AML and sanctions controls are public-law compliance duties, not a universal private reimbursement guarantee for every sender of an authorised transfer.

Evidence that matters

Build a chronological file another person can follow without relying on memory. Preserve originals where possible.

  • Transfer receipt
  • Beneficiary details
  • Reason payment was authorised
  • Date problem discovered
  • Bank case/reference number

Practical next steps

  • Contact the sending bank promptly.
  • Submit a concise chronology.
  • Ask which formal process applies.
  • Use the bank's complaint route if needed.

What not to do

  • Do not invent an unauthorised-transaction story.
  • Do not pay a third party promising a guaranteed recall.
  • Do not assume the receiving bank must return funds on request.
